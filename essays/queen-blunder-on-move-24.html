Queen Blunder on Move 24

A blunder, a clock running in another room, and a long bet on tournaments to come.

May 14, 2026 · Manny Yebz

There is a kind of move you don’t see until your hand has already left the piece. It is not the bad opening, which you saw and chose anyway, nor the slow strategic loss, which is at least honest. It is the move that is obvious — flagged in red ink by every engine on earth — and that you played without seeing, the way a man whistling on a footpath walks into a streetlamp.

Mine came on move 24. I had been playing for over an hour. The position was complicated only in a familiar way. White rooks doubled on the d-file, a queenside pawn structure I had grown up with, the kind of middlegame I have probably played eight hundred times against my brother, my cousin, the neighbour’s son who taught me what en passant was when I was nine. Every shape on the board reminded me of someone. I was, in some sense, not playing the game in front of me. I was playing every game that had ever produced this game.

I should mention that I had also, for the previous fortnight, been holding a different crisis in my left hand. The kind of thing that doesn’t appear on the score sheet. A life-shaped emergency, with its own clock running in a different room. I had been managing it by triage — twenty minutes here, an apology there, a long phone call at midnight that solved nothing but kept the wreckage upright. By the time I sat down to play, my best processing was already spoken for. The board got the leftovers.

So move 24. Black had offered a quiet pawn push — the sort of move that, in a clean head, you spot in two seconds as the prelude to a fork. My queen was sitting on a square that, in any of the eight hundred games of my childhood, was the natural square for a queen. Of course she was there. She had always been there. I moved without checking, because the position felt like home and because home is the one place we forget to look both ways. Knight to f6, a small wooden footstep, and my queen was gone.

It is the texture of a blunder I keep returning to. Not the loss of the piece — pieces come and go — but the quality of the inattention. I was not, at that moment, careless. I was preoccupied with a different board. I was being a good son and a tired brother and a person trying to hold a household together with both hands, and my chess hand, lonely, made a decision on its own. And the move it made was, in a way, also a kind of trust. The position felt safe. Familiar shapes had always rewarded me before. The whole tableau was so much like the rooms I came from that I did not bother to look at it as a stranger would, which is the only way to actually see a chessboard.

I have analysed the game since. There were three moments — three — where a single careful breath would have shown me everything. Move 19, when the knight crept up. Move 22, when the pawn structure quietly tilted. Move 23, when my opponent stopped offering trades and started arranging them. Each of these I walked past with the soft confidence of a man on his own street. Each of these, in retrospect, was a small bell I did not hear because I was on the phone with the rest of my life.

What undoes me is not the loss. Losses are weather. What undoes me is the suspicion that the position was, in fact, the best one I had been in for years — that the pieces had arranged themselves with a kind of grace I will not see twice — and that I gave it back to the table without knowing what I was holding. Some positions you only recognise as great after they are gone. Mathematicians have a word for this, I think, but it is not a kind word, and I will not use it tonight.

Still. Chess is long. The room is full of clocks. Tournaments end and tournaments begin, and the players you meet across a board have a habit of turning up again in cities you did not plan to visit, in tournaments you nearly skipped. I do not know if the queen comes back. I suspect she does not, in the strict sense, since pieces taken are taken. But I have started to believe — quietly, without insisting on it — that the game might. That somewhere in the future, on some other board, the same two players sit down again with the score reset to zero, and one of them, the slower one, the one whose hand has learned its lesson, finally takes the breath he should have taken at move 24.
