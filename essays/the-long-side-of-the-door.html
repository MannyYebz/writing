The Long Side of the Door

On thirty-one nights on a bench, a shelf in a kitchen, and the side of the door we are told is the short one.

May 17, 2026 · Manny Yebz

It is winter now and I have been sleeping on the bench outside the petrol station for thirty-one nights. I keep count because the act of counting is itself a small house, a small structure I can step inside. The body learns where to put itself even when there is nowhere to put it. The body is a very obedient student.

There is a kind of cold that arrives at the part of you that used to be most certain. I am not talking about toes. The toes are fine — they have always been honest, they tell you what they need, they accept whatever you can give them. I mean the cold that arrives at the part of you that used to know, without thinking, what time the kitchen filled with sound in the morning. The part that did not need to remember.

I want to be precise about how I came to be here, because the imprecise version sounds like a story about luck and the precise version is something else.

There was a small painted thing on a shelf in my mother’s kitchen. It had been there for as long as I had a memory to hold it in. Every morning my mother would touch it lightly with two fingers, the way you might check a sleeping child for fever, and every morning I would touch it after her, and every morning my younger sister would touch it after me. We did not speak about it. The touching was older than speech. My grandmother had set it on the shelf when the house was built. I have a photograph somewhere of her hand on it, the joints already swollen.

I cannot now reconstruct the day I stopped. There was no day. There was a long shallow slope and then a flat plain and then the shelf was something I walked past without seeing, the way a man stops seeing a streetlamp after a year of walking under it. My mother noticed before I did. She did not say anything at first. She is a careful woman and the careful are slow to speak. But the not-speaking accumulated. A house with an un-said thing in it grows heavier by a small amount each evening, and eventually you can feel the floor sagging.

What undid it, in the end, was a particular Wednesday. My uncle had come from the village — my father’s older brother, the one whose voice fills a room before he does — and we were sitting at the long table, and he reached for the bowl and waited, the way they all waited, and the silence into which he was waiting was the silence I was supposed to fill. The words sat on the shelf where the painted thing sat. They were familiar to my mouth. They had been put there when I was four. My uncle waited. My mother waited. My sister, who is younger than me by enough to still believe what she is told, looked at her plate. I waited too. I waited in the way a man waits at a fork in a road that he has already, in some quieter part of himself, walked past.

I did not say the words. I did not say anything else either. I just kept my hands in my lap.

My uncle put the bowl down very carefully. My father did not look at me. My mother began to cry without making a sound, which is the only kind of crying she has ever permitted herself in my presence. The Wednesday ended. The week ended. By the following Sunday a small bag stood beside the door, packed with clothes that smelled like my mother’s hands, and my father, who is not a cruel man but who is a man who has built his life on a particular kind of order, told me, very quietly, that he loved me, and that until the bowl could be passed again I would have to find somewhere else to put my evenings.

That was nineteen months ago. The first weeks were almost adventurous. A friend’s couch. Another friend’s couch. A small room above a barbershop where the radio played all night. The adventurous quality faded around the third month, the way the smell of a new shirt fades. By the sixth month I was on the bench.

I want to say something now that will sound strange. I am not unhappy. The unhappiness I expected has not arrived in the shape I expected it. There is a great deal of cold and a great deal of waiting in line for things, and there are nights when the bench feels less like furniture than like a long thin accusation. But the part of me I thought would collapse — the part that had been built around the touching of the painted thing — turned out to be smaller than I had feared. It was not the foundation. It was a piece of furniture in the foundation’s room. The foundation, whatever it is, has continued to hold.

What I miss is my sister. What I miss is my mother’s hand on the back of my neck when I came in late. What I miss is being known in the specific way that a family knows you, which is not, I have come to think, the same thing as being known.

I have learned that the long side of the door is not, in fact, the side I was always told it was. The long side is the inside. The inside is the side where the years are spent stacking up small certainties that turn out, when you look at them, to be a single certainty wearing different clothes. The outside, the side I now live on, is shorter than it looks. The outside is mostly just weather and the inconvenience of needing to know where the toilet is.

There is a story I will not tell my sister yet, but I will tell her one day. It is the story of a man who walked out of a warm house in a cold season and found that the warmth had not been entirely the house’s. That some of it had always been his to keep. I do not know if she will understand. I do not even know, on the bench, on most nights, if I understand it myself. But I think it. I think it, on the long side of the door, in the city my grandmother never saw, the cold arriving punctually at the part of me that used to know.
